# REFERENCE SCRIPT STUDY — Five user-supplied narration examples

**Purpose:** Extract storytelling technique for *new original scripts*; this is a writing-craft reference, not a source of factual truth and not a license to copy dialogue or narratives. The supplied reference narrations are **not reproduced verbatim** here. Claude can read the user's own transcripts in the conversation in which they were supplied, but this public-repository-safe pack contains only condensed narrative analyses.

**Correspondence warning:** The first four scripts correspond by topic to the uploaded reference videos 3, 4, 1 and 2, respectively. The fifth user-provided **railroad expansion** narration does **not** match the fifth uploaded video, which depicts a Western revolver story. Do not silently treat these as the same reference. ## Reference A — Storm-oil ship pursuit (supplied script 1)
- **Premise:** A crew member's unconventional action appears absurd while a pursuing ship and worsening storm create increasing pressure.
- **Opening hook:** Begin in the middle of a dangerous or surprising choice; protagonist action precedes explanation.
- **Beat sequence:** Action under pressure → authority challenges protagonist → approaching threat → unexpected strategic decision → escalating weather and danger → pause for a simple physical analogy → return to a climactic scene. The user-provided transcript breaks off before the complete last sentence.
- **Narration voice:** Second-person adjacent imagery ('you see' style), clipped dialogue, withheld explanation, vivid comparisons; use an original voice and phrasing in future scripts.
- **Visual plan:** Distinct silhouette for crew member, foreground oil/sea surface, deep ship/environment layers, cool/warm light contrast, clear reaction poses, gradually expanding camera scale.
- **Sound construction:** Ocean/wind bed, isolated metal-on-ship event, silence or lower music during the explanation, escalating storm sound; these are *proposed cue types*, not verified source sound timestamps.
- **Fact-check alert:** A thin oil film can affect short wind-generated capillary waves, but it does not prevent large ocean swells from breaking as dramatized. **[corrected/nuanced]** The *practice* of carrying and pouring "storm oil" is historically documented (Benjamin Franklin's 1774 pond experiments; 19th–early 20th c. seamanship and hydrographic-office guidance on oil bags). The oil film damps short waves and reduces whitecapping near the vessel; it does not flatten swell. Cite primary sources before using either point. Do NOT repeat the storm escape or giant-wave payoff as documented fact without reliable sources.

## Reference B — Japanese office isolation (supplied script 2)
- **Premise:** An apparently comfortable work assignment turns into a psychologically unsettling employment situation.
- **Opening hook:** Social/organizational paradox creating immediate curiosity; avoid overgeneralized legal claims.
- **Beat sequence:** Counterintuitive premise → workplace introduction → initial appeal → social signals of exclusion → repeated routine → missing colleague as emotional reversal → explanatory context.
- **Narration voice:** Imaginative address to viewer, compact scene-setting, occasional employer/employee speech and sharp contrast between first impression and consequence.
- **Visual plan:** Repeated room/corridor landmarks to convey time, changing expressions, clock as motif, restrained camera changes, clinical fluorescent palette.
- **Sound construction:** Office room tone, ticking motif, selective quiet, understated tension; cue descriptions are proposed not verified recordings.
- **Fact-check alert:** Japanese labor and dismissal law is nuanced. Avoid absolute language suggesting firing is impossible or all firms use this practice; verify terms, scope and specific examples.

## Reference C — Edison and hypnagogia (supplied script 3)
- **Premise:** A surprising historical anecdote becomes the entry point for an accessible neuroscience concept.
- **Opening hook:** An unexpected association between a mundane action and the potential for ideas.
- **Beat sequence:** Unusual claim → tangible physical setup → surprise sound event → historical anecdote → explain a transitional mental state → explain why the interrupted moment was meaningful → concise thematic payoff.
- **Narration voice:** Friendly, concise, explanatory questions and an accessible metaphor, with the explanation delayed until the object action becomes memorable.
- **Visual plan:** One character and central prop in a tightly composed warm workshop; cut between human reaction and a *clearly illustrative* abstraction of thinking.
- **Sound construction:** Quiet room bed, deliberate dramatic metal impact, subtle transitional ambience; proposed cues only.
- **Fact-check alert:** Historical stories about Edison can be embellished or misattributed; cite primary/credible sources. Do not present dropping objects during sleep as a recommended experiment for viewers.

## Reference D — Pachinko and prize redemption (supplied script 4)
- **Premise:** An apparent legal contradiction is gradually explained by an institution's prize arrangements.
- **Opening hook:** A paradox involving legality and an everyday commercial environment; avoid an inaccurate categorical claim.
- **Beat sequence:** Puzzle → busy environment → observer's expectation → unexpected counter result → explanatory reveal → legal ambiguity/nuance → compact closing line.
- **Narration voice:** Guided investigation with viewpoint shifts, vivid sensory verbs and escalating clues, before a concise underlying explanation.
- **Visual plan:** Neon street, aisle depth, detailed machine props, counter, exterior scene, clear scene geography; don't recreate the original shot order precisely.
- **Sound construction:** Mechanical ambience and intermittent chimes, urban transition, quieter explanation; placeholders, not source audio analysis.
- **Fact-check and age-safety alert:** Gambling regulation in Japan is more nuanced than 'all gambling is illegal.' Focus on history/legality in an educational way; do not turn this into instructions for underage gambling or exploiting rules.

## Reference E — Railroad rail gaps (supplied script 5; **no matching fifth MP4**)
- **Premise:** An unpopular engineering choice looks inferior until environmental conditions reveal a hidden physical reason.
- **Opening hook:** A visual imperfection that seems to contradict the goal of a high-quality railway.
- **Beat sequence:** Mysterious design choice → angry supervisor → rival team appears to win → social/commercial embarrassment → seasonal change → visual foreshadowing as gaps narrow → stress accumulates elsewhere → cliffhanger. **The supplied narration stops mid-action and has no final resolution.**
- **Narration voice:** Rapid cause-and-effect, opposing viewpoints, repeating sound motif, compressed months in a few scenes.
- **Visual plan:** Repeat exact rail sections across seasons, contrasting red/blue or warm/cool palettes, show deformation through a high-level safe diagram, use a scale consistent across shots.
- **Sound construction:** Repeated rail-joint rhythm as storytelling motif, swelling summer ambiance, restrained tension. These are proposed choices, not audio observations from an available matching MP4.
- **Fact-check alert:** Rails expand thermally, but joint gaps, the stated spacing and circumstances require verification. Modern continuous welded rail uses engineered stress management (rails are installed/destressed at a chosen "stress-free" or neutral temperature and restrained by ties, anchors and ballast); avoid teaching that rail joints are mandatory every fixed distance.

## Shared narrative design language (abstract rules, not copied scripts)
1. **Start in the action, not with biography.** Visualize a contradiction, small mystery, or surprising physical detail immediately.
2. **Give a human reaction.** Introduce someone who misunderstands the situation, represents conventional expectations, or raises the obvious objection.
3. **Create a pattern of escalating stakes** with 2–4 distinct visual turns. Each turn should lead to a fresh visual, not repeated exposition.
4. **Delay, then explain the mechanism.** At an appropriate point, shift from emotional storytelling to one visually legible fact or analogy.
5. **Return to the original mystery for a payoff.** Resolve the curiosity with a supported, complete ending—no cliffhanger unless intentionally chosen.
6. **Narrate for video.** Frequent concrete nouns, active verbs, short speech-like sentences, and sparse dialogue; avoid repetitive filler and generic hyperbole.
7. **Pace by beat, not fixed scene count.** Use short vivid scenes where needed; allow the mechanism a clear, unhurried explanation.
8. **Use sound as punctuation.** Music markers in user transcripts indicate editorial events but are not usable actual soundtrack timing. Build a fresh original cue sheet.
9. **Evidence before drama.** Check surprising numbers, quotes, law/history claims and physical mechanisms with reliable sources before finalizing the script.
10. **Originality check.** New topic, new story construction, original dialogue, distinct character design, different shot sequence, original/licensed audio. Do not paraphrase a whole reference script scene-by-scene.

## Recommended script deliverables per new production
- `SOURCE_NOTES.md`: supported facts, disputed claims, source links.
- `SCRIPT_BRIEF.md`: audience, single main question, visual angle, original ending, target duration.
- `SCRIPT.md`: original final voiceover, word count, estimated delivery pace, beat and caption annotations.
- `SCRIPT_BEATS.csv`: beat / time interval / narrative purpose / proof visual / emotional change.
- `AUDIO_CUES.md`: original soundtrack plan, proposed sounds, voice/caption timing.
- `SCRIPT_ORIGINALITY_AND_FACT_CHECK.md`: list of source claims, corrections, originality differences, and checks before rendering.

## Voiceover template: reusable, *not fill-in copying*
- **0–3 seconds:** Introduce the observable mystery or tension with a visual action.
- **3–10 seconds:** Establish the stakes and a character's interpretation.
- **10–25 seconds:** Reveal consequences or clues through new visual events.
- **25–40 seconds:** Explain the actual mechanism with one clear animation or analogy.
- **Final seconds:** Resolve the opening question accurately, with a unique and memorable end image.

For 30-, 45- or 60-second videos, re-time beats for real measured narration duration. Do not pretend a fixed section template is universal.
